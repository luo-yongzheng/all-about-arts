# Direction that serves the subject

Start with the desired change in the viewer: what should they understand, notice or feel? Identify factual sources for explanations and data. Preserve user-provided footage and branding when requested.

Write a short treatment before looking at demo storyboards: premise, point of view, progression, two or three visual motifs and a reason for each major shot. For data, name the comparison, units, source and uncertainty. For products, show a useful detail or action, not only an attractive rotation. For an art film, mood and a sustained composition may carry the piece without narration.

If no style is specified, offer two or three relevant choices, or select an appropriate default when the user asked for autonomous production. Explain the actual technique: pixel resolution and quantized movement, height-lit paint, layered paper, refractive geometry, drawn strokes or typographic grids. “Cinematic” alone is not a direction.

Read the chosen style's reference after this treatment. Preserve what makes the medium legible while inventing subjects, geometry, composition and actions for this film. Starter packs demonstrate a working medium and editable content; completing a commission often requires editing drawing code. Two example configs demonstrate different topics, not a guarantee that changing text produces a good film.

Test the hardest shot before building the entire film. Render a still and, for timing-dependent ideas, a short clip. Use the common time interface, object IDs and text bounds as you write original scenes. Set sound events on the same resolved timeline as the action. Evaluate the resulting shot, then scale the composition.

Pacing depends on purpose. Quiet art may need held frames; explanation needs reading and reasoning time; product film needs readable form and useful detail. Do not impose a universal shot length, required narrator, loudness of music or visual effect quota. The user's brief overrides defaults.
